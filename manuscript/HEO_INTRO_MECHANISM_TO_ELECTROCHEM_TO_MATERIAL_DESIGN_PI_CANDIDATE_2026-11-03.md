# 1. Introduction

Spinel high-entropy oxides (HEOs) combine multiple transition-metal cations within a common oxide lattice and have attracted interest as high-capacity conversion-type anodes. Although their lithium storage can be summarized by an overall oxide-to-metal/Li₂O conversion reaction, state-resolved studies reveal a more intricate evolution during lithiation. Operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy has shown that the constituent metal cations in spinel HEOs are reduced over different ranges of lithiation [7]. Ex situ X-ray absorption and diffraction studies have identified Li uptake, cation migration, and intermediate oxide structures before and during metal-forming conversion [23]. Similar intermediate oxide transformations have been observed in conventional spinel oxides such as Fe₃O₄ [22]. These studies establish that lithiation involves several overlapping reduction and structural transformation processes rather than the simultaneous conversion of all metal cations in a single step.

Knowledge of these reactions is an important starting point for materials design, but it does not by itself show how to modify an electrode to favor one reaction over another. Changes in particle size, cation composition, and Li₂O incorporation have been reported to alter the capacity and conversion behavior of spinel HEOs [9,20,24]. Although these studies relate structural evolution to electrochemical performance, the effects of materials modification are often summarized by total capacity and overall voltage profiles. As a result, how much of a capacity gain or loss arises from different parts of the conversion reaction remains unclear. This distinction matters because a modification may promote one part of lithiation, suppress another, or affect several processes together. Quantifying these changes by reaction region would make it possible to select processing and compositional modifications according to the conversion processes they enhance or limit.

The multistep nature of conversion suggests that changes in the dominant reactions should be reflected in the electrochemical response. The voltage profile provides information about reaction potentials, but the broad plateau characteristic of conversion electrodes can accommodate substantial charge without clearly separating individual processes. Overlapping reduction reactions can also produce poorly resolved differential-capacity features [7,23]. Galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) provides another dimension of information by recording voltage recovery after each current pulse. Although GITT is commonly used to estimate chemical diffusion coefficients, such estimates rely on transport and thermodynamic assumptions that become difficult to justify when reduction, structural reconstruction, and evolving interfaces contribute simultaneously [11–13]. GITT-derived electrochemical parameters have nevertheless been used to distinguish four regions with different dominant reactions in a NiO conversion anode [26]. The measured relaxation amplitude and characteristic recovery time, considered together with the rest-end voltage, therefore provide a way to identify changes in the electrochemical response without attributing the entire relaxation to a single diffusion process.

Here, first-lithiation GITT is used to compare pristine HEO, ball-milled HEO (BM-HEO), Mg-containing HEO (Mg-HEO), and ball-milled Mg-HEO (BM-Mg-HEO). Changes in rest-end voltage, relaxation amplitude, and characteristic recovery time distinguish four electrochemical regimes, whose dominant processes are interpreted using established HEO conversion chemistry. The characteristic sequence remains recognizable across the four materials, while their regional charge contributions differ substantially. Ball milling produces its largest positive regional GITT-charge increment during conversion onset and progression, whereas Mg incorporation causes its largest negative increment during later conversion. These findings show that materials modifications with opposite effects on total capacity primarily alter different parts of lithiation, providing a basis for targeting specific conversion processes through processing and compositional design.
