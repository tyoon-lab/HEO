# 1. Introduction

Spinel high-entropy oxides (HEOs) combine multiple transition-metal cations within a common oxide lattice and have attracted interest as high-capacity conversion-type anodes. Although their lithium storage can be summarized by an overall oxide-to-metal/Li₂O conversion reaction, state-resolved studies reveal a more intricate evolution during lithiation. Operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy has shown that different metal cations in a spinel HEO undergo reduction over distinct but overlapping portions of the voltage–capacity profile (Luo et al., 2022). Ex situ X-ray absorption and diffraction analyses further identify Li uptake, cation migration, and intermediate oxide reconstruction preceding and accompanying metal-forming conversion (Triolo et al., 2023). Recent multi-probe measurements have also demonstrated that particle size influences the progression and completeness of conversion in spinel HEOs (Li et al., 2025). These findings establish a physical basis for interpreting different parts of a continuous lithiation profile in terms of changes in the dominant reaction processes.

Understanding these processes provides an opportunity to control them through materials design. Processing and compositional modifications can alter reaction accessibility, cation environments, and the extent of lithiation, thereby changing the capacity delivered by an HEO anode. Indeed, Li-containing compositional changes, particle-size control, and substitution of constituent cations have been shown to modify the electrochemical behavior of spinel HEOs (Ma et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). Yet an increase or decrease in total capacity does not establish whether a modification affects the initial response, conversion onset, or the subsequent progression of reduction. The important next step is to connect the reaction chemistry established by state-resolved characterization to electrochemical signatures that can be compared across materials. Such a connection would allow the effects of processing and composition to be evaluated in terms of the reaction processes they promote or restrict, rather than capacity alone.

Voltage-profile analysis offers a direct route to this connection. Changes within particular voltage regions have been used to identify the origins of capacity loss in Si anodes (Yoon et al., 2015), and electrochemical-parameter trends from GITT have been used to distinguish regions with different dominant processes in the conversion anode NiO (Ng et al., 2021). For multication HEOs, however, overlapping reduction reactions and extended plateaus can make the relevant regions difficult to resolve from differential-capacity features alone. Galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) provides complementary information from the voltage response following successive current pulses. The amplitude and characteristic time of post-pulse voltage relaxation, considered together with the rest-end voltage, can distinguish changes in the dominant electrochemical response along lithiation. This makes it possible to relate literature-established conversion chemistry to experimentally recognizable regions and then compare how those regions respond to different materials modifications.

Here, ball milling and Mg incorporation are examined in a matched set of pristine HEO, ball-milled HEO, Mg-containing HEO, and ball-milled Mg-HEO electrodes. Their lithiation responses are divided into four recognizable electrochemical regimes from changes in GITT relaxation amplitude, characteristic time, and rest-end voltage. Guided by existing HEO reaction studies, the regimes are interpreted as early lithiation, conversion onset and progression, later conversion, and post-plateau deep lithiation. The characteristic sequence remains recognizable after modification, whereas the regional charge contributions change substantially. Ball milling produces its largest positive regional GITT-charge increment during conversion onset and progression, while Mg incorporation produces its largest negative increment during later conversion. The contrast links materials processing and cation composition to different portions of the accessible lithiation response, providing a basis for directing future modifications toward the reactions that most strongly govern capacity gains and losses.
